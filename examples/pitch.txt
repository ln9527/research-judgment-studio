Epiphanies—sudden, transformative realizations about oneself—are often cited as pivotal moments that infuse work and life with renewed purpose, yet they remain understudied in management and organizational research. While prior work has explored how purpose arises from job design, leadership, or major life transitions, less attention has been paid to how purpose can emerge suddenly even when external circumstances do not change. This gap is significant because anecdotal evidence suggests some of the most impactful shifts in identity and purpose occur unexpectedly. The authors address this by asking: What types of individuals are more likely to experience work-related epiphanies of greater strength? They propose that a specific form of mind wandering—problem-solving daydreaming—and a curiosity trait called deprivation sensitivity jointly explain variation in epiphany strength. Using survey and experimental data across three studies, including real-time assessments in leadership development contexts, they test a model linking these individual differences to epiphany strength and, in turn, to psychological tension resolution and sense of career purpose. The key claimed contribution is identifying the cognitive and dispositional foundations of powerful self-transformative experiences in professional life.
